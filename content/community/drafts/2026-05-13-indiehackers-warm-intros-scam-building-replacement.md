## Proposed response

---

Strong framing. The "class filter, not quality filter" point is the one that cuts through most cleanly — worth leading with that if you're doing any content around this.

One thing worth thinking through: the warm intro problem has two parts, and they require different solutions.

**Part 1: Discovery** (which is what Brevoir is attacking) — how does a founder with no network surface to investors at all? This is the distribution problem. Agree this is where the biggest structural inequity sits.

**Part 2: Conversion** (which is less often discussed) — once a founder gets a look, how do they compete with equally strong decks from better-networked founders? This is where the materials and follow-up process matter a lot. An investor going through 80 decks from cold sources needs to be able to go deeper with the ones that stand out without requiring a back-and-forth email chain to do it.

A few things on the distribution side that we've seen work for cold outreach:

- **Thesis-specific framing.** Don't just send the deck; open with one sentence that maps your startup to their last three investments. VCs who get cold outreach respond far more to "I noticed you led X and Y, we're doing [adjacent thing]" than generic intros.
- **Timing off announcements.** When a fund closes or announces a new portfolio company in your space, that's a brief window where they're thinking about adjacent deals. That's when to reach out cold.
- **Public signals of traction.** Post your milestones somewhere they'll find them — IH, HN, Twitter/X. It's async social proof that doesn't require a warm intro to land.

On Part 2: the single biggest lever for async conversion is knowing whether the investor actually engaged with the deck and what specifically caught their attention — which gives you something specific to say in the follow-up rather than a generic "checking in." That's the gap we've been building into at [Peeeky](https://peeeky.com) — engagement data that tells you what resonated so your follow-up doesn't feel like a cold ping.

Curious what you're seeing on the investor side: is Part 1 the dominant problem, or do you hear from founders that even when they get a look, they're struggling to convert?
